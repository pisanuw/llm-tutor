# AST 101: The Night Sky
## Lecture Plan, Example College

---

> **Curriculum Notes**
> - 4 lectures, one per week, 75 minutes each
> - Every lecture opens with a sky report and a prediction, and closes with an exit question
> - Problem Set 1 covers lectures 01 and 02; Problem Set 2 covers lectures 03 and 04
> - An observing log (five entries) runs all term, with one class observing night in week 2
> - A review session before the midterm (midterm-review.pptx) is not a lecture: it has no goals and no deck
> - No prior physics assumed; the math is ratios and angles

---

## Lecture 01: The Sky Above Us

**Week:** 1, Session 1
**Duration:** 75 min

**Topics:**
- The celestial sphere as a model of the sky
- Zenith, horizon, celestial poles and celestial equator
- Daily motion: rising and setting from Earth's rotation
- Circumpolar stars and the altitude of Polaris
- Angular measure: degrees, arcminutes, arcseconds, and the hand at arm's length

**Learning Objectives:** By the end of this lecture, students will be able to:
1. Describe the celestial sphere model and locate the celestial poles, the celestial equator, the horizon and the zenith
2. Explain the daily rising and setting of the Sun and stars as the result of Earth's rotation, and predict which stars never set from a given latitude
3. Estimate angular sizes and separations on the sky with an outstretched hand, and convert between degrees, arcminutes and arcseconds

**Time Allocation:**
| Segment | Minutes |
|---------|---------|
| Welcome and first sky report | 5 |
| Prediction: where is Polaris? | 5 |
| The celestial sphere (umbrella model) | 15 |
| Daily motion and circumpolar stars | 20 |
| Measuring angles with your hand (pair activity) | 20 |
| Summary, exit question, observing task | 10 |

**Key Examples:**
- The umbrella with stars painted inside, handle at the celestial pole
- The office chair: spin and the room appears to turn
- The Big Dipper, circumpolar from latitude 45 degrees north
- Hand at arm's length: little finger 1 degree, fist 10 degrees

**Demonstrations:**
1. Students point to the zenith, the horizon and where they think Polaris is, then compare with a sky chart
2. Office-chair spin: watch the room "rise" and "set"
3. Pairs measure the angle between two marks on the far wall with their hands, then check with a protractor

**Assignment Connection:** Problem Set 1, questions 1 to 3 (angles and daily motion); observing log entry 1 (find Polaris and the Big Dipper).

**Prerequisite Lectures:** None

## Lecture 02: Phases of the Moon and Eclipses

**Week:** 2, Session 1
**Duration:** 75 min

**Topics:**
- What causes the Moon's phases (and what does not)
- The 29.5-day cycle and the names of the phases
- Moonrise and moonset times by phase
- Solar and lunar eclipses; the 5-degree tilt of the Moon's orbit and eclipse seasons
- Tides: two bulges and spring and neap tides

**Learning Objectives:** By the end of this lecture, students will be able to:
1. Explain the Moon's phases from the changing angle between the Sun, the Moon and Earth, and rule out Earth's shadow as the cause
2. State when a solar or lunar eclipse can happen, using the Moon's phase and the tilt of its orbit, and explain why eclipses do not happen every month
3. Explain the two daily high tides from the difference in the Moon's pull across Earth, and say why spring tides happen at new and full moon

**Time Allocation:**
| Segment | Minutes |
|---------|---------|
| Sky report and recap question | 5 |
| Prediction poll: why does the Moon change shape? | 5 |
| Lamp-and-ball activity and the cause of phases | 20 |
| Rise times and the phase cycle | 10 |
| Eclipses and eclipse seasons | 15 |
| Tides | 10 |
| Summary and exit question | 10 |

**Key Examples:**
- The lamp and the ball: the Moon is always half lit
- The crescent Moon near the setting Sun, which Earth's shadow cannot explain
- Two hula hoops tilted 5 degrees: the nodes
- Three runners on one rope: why there are two tidal bulges

**Demonstrations:**
1. Every student holds a ball at arm's length and turns in a circle around a bare lamp
2. Two tilted hoops show why most new moons pass above or below the Sun
3. A stretched rubber sheet shows the near and far tidal bulges

**Assignment Connection:** Problem Set 1, questions 4 to 6 (phases, rise times, eclipses); observing log entry 2 (the Moon's phase and position at sunset on three nights).

**Prerequisite Lectures:** 01

## Lecture 03: Seasons

**Week:** 3, Session 1
**Duration:** 75 min

**Topics:**
- Why the Earth-Sun distance does not cause the seasons
- The 23.5-degree axial tilt and its two effects: angle of sunlight and day length
- Solstices, equinoxes, the tropics and the polar circles
- Seasonal lag
- The Sun's daily path and its noon altitude through the year

**Learning Objectives:** By the end of this lecture, students will be able to:
1. Explain the seasons from the tilt of Earth's axis, through the angle of sunlight and the length of the day, and rule out the changing Earth-Sun distance as the cause
2. Identify the solstices and equinoxes on a diagram of Earth's orbit and state the Sun's position and the length of the day at each
3. Predict where the Sun rises and sets and how high it climbs at noon on a given date and latitude

**Time Allocation:**
| Segment | Minutes |
|---------|---------|
| Sky report and recap question | 5 |
| Prediction: why is summer hot? | 5 |
| The distance myth and the January perihelion | 10 |
| Tilt: the flashlight demonstration and day length | 20 |
| Solstices, equinoxes and seasonal lag | 15 |
| The Sun's path and noon altitude (pair problem) | 10 |
| Summary and exit question | 10 |

**Key Examples:**
- The flashlight on the table: the same light spread over more area
- Earth is closest to the Sun in early January
- Australia's summer is in December
- The noon Sun at 47 degrees north: 66.5 degrees up in June, 19.5 degrees in December

**Demonstrations:**
1. A flashlight shone straight down and then tilted onto graph paper; students count the squares lit
2. A tilted globe carried around a lamp, axis always pointing the same way
3. A sun-path dome (a clear bowl) traced with a marker for June, March and December

**Assignment Connection:** Problem Set 2, questions 1 to 3 (seasons and the noon Sun); observing log entry 3 (where the Sun sets, against a fixed landmark).

**Prerequisite Lectures:** 01

## Lecture 04: Light and Telescopes

**Week:** 4, Session 1
**Duration:** 75 min

**Topics:**
- Light as an electromagnetic wave: wavelength, frequency, energy
- The electromagnetic spectrum and the atmosphere's windows
- Color and temperature
- The inverse-square law for brightness
- Telescopes: light-gathering power, resolution, and why magnification matters least

**Learning Objectives:** By the end of this lecture, students will be able to:
1. Order the bands of the electromagnetic spectrum by wavelength, frequency and photon energy, and relate wavelength to color
2. Use the inverse-square law to compute how the apparent brightness of a source changes when its distance changes
3. Explain why a telescope's light-gathering power grows with the square of its aperture and how aperture limits the finest detail it can resolve

**Time Allocation:**
| Segment | Minutes |
|---------|---------|
| Sky report and recap question | 5 |
| Light as a wave and the spectrum | 15 |
| Prediction and demonstration: twice as far | 5 |
| The inverse-square law (pair problem) | 15 |
| Prediction: what is a telescope for? | 5 |
| Light-gathering power and resolution | 15 |
| Summary, exit question, course wrap-up | 15 |

**Key Examples:**
- The piano keyboard: our eyes see one octave
- The flashlight beam spreading over four times the area at twice the distance
- Identical streetlights along a road
- The bucket in the rain: a 2-meter telescope versus a 7-millimeter pupil

**Demonstrations:**
1. A prism and a white lamp; then a remote control seen through a phone camera (infrared)
2. A flashlight through a square cutout onto a grid at one and two distances
3. Two telescopes side by side on the same faint target

**Assignment Connection:** Problem Set 2, questions 4 to 6 (spectrum, inverse-square law, aperture); observing log entry 5 (the Moon through the class telescope).

**Prerequisite Lectures:** 01
