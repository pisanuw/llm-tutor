# AST 102: Stars and Galaxies
## Lecture Plan, Example College

---

> **Curriculum Notes**
> - 4 lectures, one per week, 75 minutes each
> - Follows AST 101; assumes angular measure, the spectrum and the inverse-square law from AST 101 lectures 01 and 04
> - Every lecture opens with a sky report and a prediction, and closes with an exit question
> - Problem Set 1 covers all four lectures, released in two halves (weeks 1 and 3)

## Lecture 01: Measuring the Stars

**Week:** 1, Session 1
**Duration:** 75 min

**Topics:**
- The distance problem: every star is a point of light
- Stellar parallax and the parsec
- Why parallax only reaches nearby stars
- The magnitude scale: apparent and absolute magnitude
- Color, temperature and the spectral classes O B A F G K M

**Learning Objectives:** By the end of this lecture, students will be able to:
1. Compute a star's distance in parsecs from its parallax angle in arcseconds, and explain why parallax only works for nearby stars
2. Compare the brightness of two stars from their magnitudes, remembering that the scale runs backwards and that 5 magnitudes is a factor of 100
3. Order the spectral classes O B A F G K M by surface temperature and color, and place the Sun among them

**Time Allocation:**
| Segment | Minutes |
|---------|---------|
| Sky report and recap from AST 101 | 5 |
| Prediction and demonstration: the thumb blink | 10 |
| Parallax and the parsec (pair problem) | 20 |
| Magnitudes | 15 |
| Color, temperature and spectral classes | 15 |
| Summary and exit question | 10 |

**Key Examples:**
- The thumb blink: nearer objects jump more
- Proxima Centauri: parallax 0.77 arcseconds, about 1.3 parsecs
- Golf scores: lower is better
- The stove burner glowing red, then orange, then white

**Demonstrations:**
1. Thumb blink at two arm positions, then a meter stick held at two distances
2. Two identical lamps at different distances, ranked by eye, then by a phone light meter
3. A dimmer-controlled bulb seen through a diffraction grating as it heats up

**Assignment Connection:** Problem Set 1 (AST 102), questions 1 to 3 (parallax, magnitudes, spectral classes).

**Prerequisite Lectures:** None

## Lecture 02: The Lives of Stars

**Week:** 2, Session 1
**Duration:** 75 min

**Topics:**
- Luminosity and temperature: two numbers per star
- The Hertzsprung-Russell diagram: main sequence, giants, white dwarfs
- Hydrogen fusion and the balance between gravity and pressure
- Mass and lifetime on the main sequence
- Stellar endpoints: white dwarfs, neutron stars and black holes

**Learning Objectives:** By the end of this lecture, students will be able to:
1. Place a star on the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram from its temperature and luminosity, and name the main sequence, giants and white dwarfs
2. Explain how hydrogen fusion in the core powers a main-sequence star and why more massive stars burn out faster
3. Predict whether a star ends as a white dwarf, a neutron star or a black hole from its initial mass

**Time Allocation:**
| Segment | Minutes |
|---------|---------|
| Sky report and recap question | 5 |
| Building the H-R diagram from a star list | 20 |
| Prediction: why do stars shine? | 5 |
| Fusion and hydrostatic balance | 15 |
| Lifetimes and endpoints | 20 |
| Summary and exit question | 10 |

**Key Examples:**
- A class "census" plot of height against shoe size: most points fall on one band
- Betelgeuse (cool, luminous, huge) and Sirius B (hot, dim, tiny)
- The gas-guzzler: bigger tank, much bigger engine
- The Sun: about 10 billion years on the main sequence, then a red giant, then a white dwarf

**Demonstrations:**
1. Students place twenty star cards on a wall-sized H-R grid
2. A balloon held between two hands: gravity squeezing, pressure pushing back
3. A sorting game: star masses into white dwarf, neutron star and black hole bins

**Assignment Connection:** Problem Set 1 (AST 102), question 4 (the H-R diagram and stellar lifetimes).

**Prerequisite Lectures:** 01

## Lecture 03: Galaxies

**Week:** 3, Session 1
**Duration:** 75 min

**Topics:**
- The Milky Way: disk, bulge, halo, and where the Sun is
- Why the Milky Way looks like a band across the sky
- Other galaxies and the Andromeda galaxy
- Spiral, elliptical and irregular galaxies
- Rotation curves and the evidence for dark matter

**Learning Objectives:** By the end of this lecture, students will be able to:
1. Describe the Milky Way's disk, bulge and halo, where the Sun sits in it, and why we see the galaxy as a band across the sky
2. Classify a galaxy as spiral, elliptical or irregular from an image and relate each type to its stars and gas
3. Explain why flat galaxy rotation curves imply more mass than the visible stars and gas, and what that unseen mass is called

**Time Allocation:**
| Segment | Minutes |
|---------|---------|
| Sky report and recap question | 5 |
| Prediction: what is the band of the Milky Way? | 5 |
| Structure of our galaxy | 15 |
| Galaxy sorting activity | 15 |
| Rotation curves and dark matter | 25 |
| Summary and exit question | 10 |

**Key Examples:**
- Inside the forest: why the disk looks like a band
- The Sun about 26,000 light-years from the center, orbiting at about 230 km/s
- Andromeda, about 2.5 million light-years away, visible to the eye from a dark site
- The ball on a string: faster orbits need a stronger pull

**Demonstrations:**
1. Students stand in a "disk" of classmates and describe what they see along it and above it
2. Pairs sort thirty galaxy images into three piles, then justify one hard case
3. Swinging a ball on a string at two speeds, then comparing the solar system's falling curve with a galaxy's flat one

**Assignment Connection:** Problem Set 1 (AST 102), question 5 (galaxy types and the rotation curve).

**Prerequisite Lectures:** 01

## Lecture 04: The Expanding Universe

**Week:** 4, Session 1
**Duration:** 75 min

**Topics:**
- The Doppler effect and redshift
- Measuring a redshift from a spectral line
- Hubble's law and the Hubble constant
- Expansion without a center; the age of the universe
- The evidence for the Big Bang: expansion, the cosmic microwave background, light elements

**Learning Objectives:** By the end of this lecture, students will be able to:
1. Explain redshift as the stretching of light's wavelength and compute a redshift from an observed and a rest wavelength
2. Use Hubble's law (velocity equals the Hubble constant times distance) to estimate a galaxy's distance from its recession velocity
3. List the main evidence for the Big Bang (the expansion, the cosmic microwave background, the abundance of light elements) and say what each shows

**Time Allocation:**
| Segment | Minutes |
|---------|---------|
| Sky report and recap question | 5 |
| Prediction: a siren passes | 5 |
| Redshift (pair problem with a real spectrum) | 15 |
| Hubble's law and distances | 15 |
| Raisin bread: no center, and the age of the universe | 10 |
| The evidence for the Big Bang | 15 |
| Summary, exit question, course wrap-up | 10 |

**Key Examples:**
- The passing siren
- The hydrogen-alpha line, rest wavelength 656.3 nm, observed at 662.9 nm
- A galaxy receding at 7,000 km/s is about 100 megaparsecs away
- Raisin bread rising: every raisin sees the others move away

**Demonstrations:**
1. A buzzer on a string swung in a circle
2. A balloon with dots drawn on it, measured with string as it inflates
3. A "baby picture" of the universe: the microwave background map, compared with a photograph of a glowing stove element

**Assignment Connection:** Problem Set 1 (AST 102), questions 6 and 7 (redshift and Hubble's law).

**Prerequisite Lectures:** 03
