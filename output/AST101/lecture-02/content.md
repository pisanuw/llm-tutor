## Slide 4: What Causes the Phases?
type: content
title: What Causes the Phases?
bullets:
  - The Sun always lights exactly half of the Moon
  - As the Moon orbits Earth, we see different fractions of that lit half
  - The phase depends only on the angle between the Sun, the Moon and Earth
  - Earth's shadow plays no part (except during a lunar eclipse)
body: |
  The Moon is always half lit. The question is how much of the lit half faces you.
notes: Say the body line twice. The test of the shadow idea: a crescent Moon sits close to the Sun in the sky, but Earth's shadow points away from the Sun.

## Slide 5: The Lamp-and-Ball Model
type: content
title: The Lamp-and-Ball Model
bullets:
  - The bare lamp is the Sun; the ball on a stick is the Moon; your head is Earth
  - Hold the ball at arm's length and turn slowly in a circle
  - Ball toward the lamp: you see its dark side (new moon)
  - Ball opposite the lamp: you see its whole lit side (full moon)
notes: Everyone does this, lights off. Point out that their head's shadow never touches the ball except at full, when the ball passes directly behind it.

## Slide 6: The Cycle of Phases
type: content
title: The Cycle of Phases
bullets:
  - New, waxing crescent, first quarter, waxing gibbous
  - Full, waning gibbous, third quarter, waning crescent
  - About 29.5 days from one new moon to the next
  - "Waxing: the lit part grows. Waning: it shrinks"
body: |
  Top-down view from above the North Pole (Sun far to the left,
  Moon moving counterclockwise):

             third quarter
                   o
  Sun -->   new o  E  o full
                   o
             first quarter
notes: The quarter phases look like half Moons. The name means a quarter of the way around the orbit, not a quarter lit.

## Slide 7: When Does the Moon Rise?
type: content
title: When Does the Moon Rise?
bullets:
  - New moon rises with the Sun, at sunrise, and is lost in its glare
  - First quarter rises around noon and is highest at sunset
  - Full moon rises at sunset and sets at sunrise
  - Third quarter rises around midnight and is up in the morning sky
notes: The Moon is not a night-only object. A first quarter Moon in the afternoon sky is a great free demonstration.

## Slide 8: Solar and Lunar Eclipses
type: content
title: Solar and Lunar Eclipses
bullets:
  - "Solar eclipse: the Moon passes between the Sun and Earth, so only at new moon"
  - "Lunar eclipse: the Moon passes into Earth's shadow, so only at full moon"
  - A lunar eclipse is visible from the whole night side of Earth
  - A total solar eclipse is visible only along a narrow path
notes: Eye safety. Never look at the Sun, eclipse or not, without a proper solar filter. Sunglasses are not a solar filter.

## Slide 10: Why Not Every Month?
type: content
title: Why Not Every Month?
bullets:
  - The Moon's orbit is tilted about 5 degrees to Earth's orbit
  - Most months the new moon passes a little above or below the Sun
  - And the full moon passes above or below Earth's shadow
  - An eclipse needs the Moon at a node, where the two orbits cross, at new or full moon
body: |
  Two hula hoops, tilted 5 degrees, crossing at two points: the nodes.
notes: Do the hoops demonstration. The line of nodes points at the Sun about twice a year.

---

## Slide 11: Eclipse Seasons
type: content
title: Eclipse Seasons
bullets:
  - The nodes line up with the Sun about every six months
  - Each lineup opens an eclipse season of about a month
  - So eclipses come in pairs or small groups, roughly twice a year
  - Every year has at least two solar eclipses somewhere on Earth, but most are partial
notes: Eclipses are predictable centuries ahead. Predict them instead of waiting to hear about them.

---

## Slide 12: Totality and the Shadow
type: content
title: Totality and the Shadow
bullets:
  - "**Umbra:** the dark inner shadow; inside it the Sun is fully covered"
  - "**Penumbra:** the lighter outer shadow; inside it the eclipse is partial"
  - When the Moon is near its farthest point, it cannot cover the whole Sun
  - That leaves a ring of sunlight: an annular eclipse
notes: The Moon and the Sun look almost exactly the same size in our sky, about half a degree. A lucky coincidence.

## Slide 14: Two Bulges, Two Tides
type: content
title: Two Bulges, Two Tides
bullets:
  - The Moon's gravity pulls hardest on the side of Earth nearest it
  - It pulls less on Earth's center, and least on the far side
  - That difference stretches Earth and its oceans into two bulges
  - Earth turns under both bulges, so most coasts get two high tides a day
body: |
  Three runners on one rope, pulled from the front: the group stretches.
notes: The far-side bulge is the surprise. It is not pushed; it is left behind.

---

## Slide 15: Spring and Neap Tides
type: content
title: Spring and Neap Tides
bullets:
  - The Sun raises tides too, a bit under half as strong as the Moon's
  - At new and full moon the Sun and Moon line up and their tides add
  - "Spring tides: the biggest range (nothing to do with the season)"
  - "Neap tides: at the quarter phases, the smallest range"
notes: Connects straight back to the phases. Same geometry, different consequence.

---

## Slide 16: Summary
type: summary
title: Summary
bullets:
  - Phases come from the Sun-Earth-Moon angle; the Moon is always half lit
  - Solar eclipses need a new moon, lunar eclipses a full moon, and both need the Moon at a node
  - The 5-degree tilt is why eclipses come about twice a year, not monthly
  - Two tidal bulges give two high tides a day; spring tides come at new and full moon
