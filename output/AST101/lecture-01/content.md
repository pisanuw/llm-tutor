## Slide 3: The Celestial Sphere
type: content
title: The Celestial Sphere
bullets:
  - Picture the sky as a huge sphere with you at the center
  - Every star sits somewhere on its inside surface
  - It is a model, not a real shell; the stars are at very different distances
  - We still use it because it tells you exactly where to look
body: |
  Think of an open umbrella over your head with stars painted on the inside.
notes: The umbrella is the image to hold on to all term. The handle will matter in a few slides.

## Slide 6: Why the Sky Turns
type: content
title: Why the Sky Turns
bullets:
  - Earth spins west to east once a day
  - So the whole sky appears to turn east to west around the celestial poles
  - About 15 degrees per hour (360 degrees in 24 hours)
  - The Sun, the Moon and the stars all rise in the east and set in the west for the same reason
body: |
  Spin slowly in an office chair. The room goes around you, and nothing in the room moved.
notes: The stars are not moving across the sky. We are turning underneath them.

---

## Slide 7: Circumpolar Stars
type: content
title: Circumpolar Stars
bullets:
  - Polaris is almost exactly at the north celestial pole, so it barely moves
  - Its altitude above your horizon equals your latitude
  - Stars closer to the pole than that angle circle it without ever setting
  - From 45 degrees north, the Big Dipper is up all night, every night
notes: Back to the tally from slide 5. Polaris is not overhead unless you live at the North Pole, and it is not the brightest star either.

## Slide 8: Check: Which Stars Never Set?
type: quiz
title: Check: Which Stars Never Set?
bullets:
  - You are at latitude 30 degrees north
  - How high above the northern horizon is Polaris?
  - Which stars never set for you, in degrees from the pole?
  - What changes if you travel to the equator?
notes: Answers are 30 degrees, stars within 30 degrees of the pole, and at the equator Polaris sits on the horizon so no stars are circumpolar.

## Slide 9: Measuring Angles With Your Hand
type: content
title: Measuring Angles With Your Hand
bullets:
  - Hold your hand at arm's length
  - Little finger is about 1 degree wide; the full Moon is half that
  - Three middle fingers together are about 5 degrees
  - A closed fist is about 10 degrees; a spread hand about 20
body: |
  Your hand and your arm grow together, so this works for almost everyone.
notes: Pairs measure the angle between two marks on the back wall, then check with the protractor.

## Slide 10: Degrees, Arcminutes, Arcseconds
type: content
title: Degrees, Arcminutes, Arcseconds
bullets:
  - One degree = 60 arcminutes
  - One arcminute = 60 arcseconds
  - So one degree = 3,600 arcseconds
  - The full Moon is about 30 arcminutes across; a star's parallax is under 1 arcsecond
notes: Same pattern as hours, minutes and seconds. The tiny arcsecond comes back in AST 102 when we measure star distances.

---

## Slide 11: Try It: The Big Dipper
type: quiz
title: Try It: The Big Dipper
bullets:
  - The two pointer stars of the Big Dipper look half a fist apart
  - Estimate their separation in degrees
  - Convert it to arcminutes
  - Now follow the pointers about five times that distance. What do you find?
notes: About 5 degrees, so 300 arcminutes. Five times along the pointers lands near Polaris.

## Slide 12: Common Mix-Ups
type: content
title: Common Mix-Ups
bullets:
  - "The stars are on a real dome at one distance: no, the sphere is a model"
  - "Polaris is the brightest star: no, it is about the 50th"
  - "The stars move across the sky at night: no, Earth turns"
  - "An angle on the sky tells you a size: only if you also know the distance"
notes: Each one is a good wrong answer. Ask who held it before today.

## Slide 13: Summary
type: summary
title: Summary
bullets:
  - The celestial sphere is a model centered on you, with poles, equator, horizon and zenith
  - Earth's rotation makes the whole sky turn about 15 degrees per hour
  - Polaris sits at an altitude equal to your latitude; stars near it never set
  - Your hand at arm's length measures angles; 1 degree = 60 arcminutes = 3,600 arcseconds
