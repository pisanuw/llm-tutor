## Slide 3: Light Is a Wave
type: content
title: Light Is a Wave
bullets:
  - Light is an electromagnetic wave traveling at about 300,000 km/s
  - "**Wavelength:** the distance from one crest to the next"
  - "**Frequency:** how many crests pass each second"
  - Wavelength times frequency equals the speed of light, so short waves mean high frequency
notes: Light also arrives in packets called photons. A shorter wavelength photon carries more energy. That is why ultraviolet burns and radio does not.

---

## Slide 4: The Spectrum
type: content
title: The Spectrum
bullets:
  - From longest to shortest wavelength: radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray, gamma ray
  - Visible light runs from red (about 700 nm) to violet (about 400 nm)
  - Shorter wavelength means higher frequency and more energy per photon
  - Our eyes see only a thin slice of the whole spectrum
body: |
  A piano keyboard miles long, and our eyes hear about one octave of it.
notes: Prism demonstration, then a TV remote seen through a phone camera. The camera sees infrared that your eyes cannot.

## Slide 5: Color and Temperature
type: content
title: Color and Temperature
bullets:
  - Every warm object glows; the hotter it is, the shorter the wavelength of its peak glow
  - A stove burner goes dull red, orange, then yellow-white as it heats
  - You glow too, in the infrared, at about 10 micrometers
  - So a star's color is a thermometer, which we will use in AST 102
notes: Red means cool here. The faucet labels have it backwards for astronomy.

---

## Slide 6: Our Atmosphere's Windows
type: content
title: Our Atmosphere's Windows
bullets:
  - Visible light and most radio waves pass through the air to the ground
  - Much of the infrared is absorbed by water vapor
  - Ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays are blocked, which is good news for us
  - To see in those bands, telescopes have to go to space
notes: That is why radio dishes sit on the ground and X-ray telescopes are satellites.

## Slide 8: Brightness Falls Off With Distance
type: content
title: Brightness Falls Off With Distance
bullets:
  - Light spreads out over a sphere whose area grows as the distance squared
  - At twice the distance, the same light covers four times the area
  - So apparent brightness goes as 1 over distance squared
  - Three times as far, one ninth as bright; ten times as far, one hundredth
body: |
  A flashlight beam at twice the distance lights four times as many squares, each a quarter as bright.
notes: This is the inverse-square law. Ratios only today; no watts needed.

## Slide 9: Try It: Streetlights
type: quiz
title: Try It: Streetlights
bullets:
  - Two identical streetlights stand along a straight road
  - The far one looks one sixteenth as bright as the near one
  - How many times farther away is it?
  - Now flip it: a light four times closer looks how much brighter?
notes: Four times farther, because 4 squared is 16. Four times closer looks 16 times brighter.

## Slide 11: Two Kinds of Telescope
type: content
title: Two Kinds of Telescope
bullets:
  - "**Refractor:** a lens at the front bends light to a focus"
  - "**Reflector:** a curved mirror at the back reflects light to a focus"
  - Every large research telescope is a reflector; mirrors can be supported from behind
  - Either way, the opening that collects light is the aperture
notes: Big lenses sag under their own weight. Mirrors can be propped up.

---

## Slide 12: Bigger Is Better
type: content
title: Bigger Is Better
bullets:
  - Light-gathering power grows with the area of the aperture, so with its diameter squared
  - A telescope twice as wide collects four times as much light
  - Resolution, the finest detail you can see, also gets better with a bigger aperture
  - Magnification only enlarges what the aperture collected and resolved
body: |
  A telescope is a bucket for light. A bucket twice as wide catches four times the rain.
notes: A 2-meter mirror against your 7-millimeter pupil: about 80,000 times the light.

---

## Slide 13: Magnification Is the Least Important
type: content
title: Magnification Is the Least Important
bullets:
  - Any telescope can magnify; most stars stay points at any magnification
  - Too much magnification just spreads a faint, blurry image over a bigger area
  - Faint galaxies need light-gathering power, not magnification
  - Fine detail on planets needs resolution, which also comes from aperture
notes: Big image of a little light is a big blur.

## Slide 14: Check: Compare Two Telescopes
type: quiz
title: Check: Compare Two Telescopes
bullets:
  - Telescope A has a 10 cm aperture and 300x magnification
  - Telescope B has a 30 cm aperture and 100x magnification
  - Which one shows fainter galaxies, and by how much more light?
  - Which one shows finer detail?
notes: B, nine times the light, and three times finer detail. You can always change the eyepiece; you cannot change the aperture.

## Slide 15: Summary
type: summary
title: Summary
bullets:
  - Light is an electromagnetic wave; shorter wavelength means higher frequency and energy
  - The spectrum runs radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray, gamma ray
  - Brightness falls as 1 over distance squared
  - Aperture rules: light-gathering power goes as diameter squared, and resolution improves with it
