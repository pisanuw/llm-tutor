## Slide 4: The Distance Myth
type: content
title: The Distance Myth
bullets:
  - Earth's orbit is nearly a circle; its distance varies only about 3 percent
  - Earth is closest to the Sun (about 147 million km) in early January
  - And farthest (about 152 million km) in early July
  - When it is summer in the north, it is winter in Australia, at the same distance
notes: Two killer facts. If distance ran the seasons, the whole planet would have summer at once, and the north would have it in January.

---

## Slide 5: Tilt, Not Distance
type: content
title: Tilt, Not Distance
bullets:
  - Earth's axis is tilted 23.5 degrees from upright, relative to its orbit
  - The axis keeps pointing the same way in space all year
  - In June the Northern Hemisphere leans toward the Sun; in December, away
  - Leaning toward the Sun means more direct sunlight and longer days
notes: Carry the tilted globe around the lamp. The axis does not wobble back and forth during the year; that is the point.

## Slide 6: The Flashlight on the Table
type: content
title: The Flashlight on the Table
bullets:
  - Shine a flashlight straight down: a small, bright circle
  - Tilt it: the same light spreads over a larger, dimmer oval
  - High summer Sun is the straight beam; low winter Sun is the tilted one
  - Same energy, more area, less heating per square meter
body: |
  Count the squares of graph paper each beam lights. That count is the whole story.
notes: Students do this in pairs. The tilted beam usually lights about twice as many squares.

## Slide 7: Longer Days, Shorter Nights
type: content
title: Longer Days, Shorter Nights
bullets:
  - The hemisphere tilted toward the Sun spends more of each rotation in daylight
  - At 47 degrees north: about 16 hours of daylight in June, about 8.5 in December
  - More hours of sunshine plus more direct light: summer
  - Both effects come from the same tilt
notes: Two effects, one cause. Students often remember one and forget the other.

## Slide 8: Check: Seasons in Australia
type: quiz
title: Check: Seasons in Australia
bullets:

  - What season is it in Sydney? In Chicago?
  - Which way is each hemisphere leaning?
  - What does this tell you about the distance idea?
notes: Summer in Sydney, winter in Chicago, same Earth-Sun distance. Distance cannot be the cause.

## Slide 9: Solstices and Equinoxes
type: content
title: Solstices and Equinoxes
bullets:
  - "June solstice (about June 21): the Sun is farthest north; longest day in the Northern Hemisphere"
  - "December solstice (about December 21): the Sun is farthest south; shortest northern day"
  - "Equinoxes (about March 20 and September 22): the Sun is over the equator"
  - At the equinoxes, day and night are about 12 hours each, everywhere on Earth
notes: Solstice means the Sun stands still: its noon height stops rising and turns back. Draw the four positions on the orbit diagram.

## Slide 10: The Tropics and the Arctic Circle
type: content
title: The Tropics and the Arctic Circle
bullets:
  - The Sun can be straight overhead only between 23.5 degrees north and south
  - Those lines are the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn
  - North of 66.5 degrees (the Arctic Circle) the Sun does not set at the June solstice
  - Same tilt, same 23.5 degrees, showing up on every map
notes: Point out that 90 minus 23.5 is 66.5. These map lines are astronomy.

## Slide 11: Why the Hottest Days Come Late
type: content
title: Why the Hottest Days Come Late
bullets:
  - The most sunlight arrives at the June solstice
  - But the hottest weather usually comes in July or August
  - Land and especially oceans take weeks to warm up and to cool down
  - This seasonal lag is like a pot on a stove: it keeps heating after you turn the burner down
notes: Same reason the hottest part of the day is mid-afternoon, not noon.

## Slide 12: Try It: Noon Sun Height
type: quiz
title: Try It: Noon Sun Height
bullets:
  - "Rule: noon altitude = 90 degrees - latitude + Sun's declination"
  - "Declination: +23.5 in June, 0 at the equinoxes, -23.5 in December"
  - Find the noon Sun's altitude at 47 degrees north in June and in December
  - Which one casts the longer shadow?
notes: June 66.5 degrees, December 19.5 degrees. The December shadow is far longer.

## Slide 13: The Sun's Path Across the Sky
type: content
title: The Sun's Path Across the Sky
bullets:
  - Every day the Sun follows an arc from the eastern horizon to the western
  - In summer the arc is long and high; in winter it is short and low
  - From the northern mid-latitudes the noon Sun is always due south
  - It is never straight overhead north of the Tropic of Cancer
body: |
  Mark where noon sunlight from a window lands on the floor once a month. Watch it move.
notes: Trace the June, March and December arcs on the clear bowl.

## Slide 14: Sunrise and Sunset Points
type: content
title: Sunrise and Sunset Points
bullets:
  - At the equinoxes the Sun rises due east and sets due west, everywhere
  - In June (northern summer) it rises north of east and sets north of west
  - In December it rises south of east and sets south of west
  - Over a year, the sunset point slides back and forth along your horizon
notes: Observing log entry 3: pick a landmark and record where the Sun sets, twice, two weeks apart.

## Slide 15: Summary
type: summary
title: Summary
bullets:
  - Seasons come from the 23.5-degree tilt, not from Earth's distance to the Sun
  - Tilt gives more direct sunlight and longer days to the hemisphere leaning sunward
  - Solstices mark the Sun's farthest north and south; equinoxes put it over the equator
  - Noon altitude = 90 degrees - latitude + declination; the Sun rises north of east in summer
