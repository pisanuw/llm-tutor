## Slide 3: The Distance Problem
type: content
title: The Distance Problem
bullets:
  - Every star except the Sun is a point of light, even in big telescopes
  - A faint star might be a dim star nearby or a brilliant star far away
  - We cannot go there, and we cannot bounce radar off a star
  - What we can do: measure angles very, very carefully
notes: Recap from AST 101: angles, arcseconds, and the inverse-square law. All three come back today.

## Slide 5: Parallax
type: content
title: Parallax
bullets:
  - Parallax is the apparent shift of a nearby object against a distant background
  - Earth's orbit gives us two viewpoints, six months apart
  - A nearby star shifts back and forth against much farther stars
  - The parallax angle p is half of the total shift; nearer stars have bigger p
body: |
  Your eyes are the two ends of Earth's orbit. Your thumb is a very small star.
notes: Draw the triangle. Sun at the base, Earth on either side, star at the tip. The angle at the tip is p.

---

## Slide 6: The Parsec
type: content
title: The Parsec
bullets:
  - A star with a parallax of 1 arcsecond is 1 parsec away
  - "Distance in parsecs = 1 / parallax in arcseconds, or d = 1/p"
  - One parsec is about 3.26 light-years, or about 206,000 times the Earth-Sun distance
  - Half the parallax means twice the distance
notes: The name is parallax plus arcsecond. Astronomers made a unit just to make this formula one division.

notes: About 1.3 parsecs, about 4.2 light-years. More than 1 parsec, because p is less than 1 arcsecond.

## Slide 8: Why Parallax Runs Out
type: content
title: Why Parallax Runs Out
bullets:
  - Even the nearest star has a parallax under 1 arcsecond
  - A star 100 parsecs away shifts only 0.01 arcseconds
  - Our atmosphere blurs images by about 1 arcsecond, so the best parallaxes come from space
  - Space missions reach thousands of parsecs, still a small corner of our galaxy
notes: Parallax is the first rung of the distance ladder. Every method for farther objects is calibrated on it.

## Slide 9: Magnitudes
type: content
title: Magnitudes
bullets:
  - Brightness on the sky is measured in magnitudes
  - The scale runs backwards; a smaller number means a brighter star
  - Five magnitudes is exactly a factor of 100 in brightness
  - One magnitude is a factor of about 2.5
body: |
  Like golf scores: lower is better.
notes: The scale comes from ancient star catalogs that ranked the brightest stars "first magnitude". Blame them, then learn it anyway.

---

## Slide 10: Apparent and Absolute Magnitude
type: content
title: Apparent and Absolute Magnitude
bullets:
  - "**Apparent magnitude:** how bright a star looks from Earth"
  - "**Absolute magnitude:** how bright it would look from 10 parsecs"
  - Absolute magnitude compares the stars' true power on a fair footing
  - The Sun is apparent magnitude about -27 but absolute magnitude about 4.8
notes: From 10 parsecs the Sun would be an unremarkable naked-eye star. The inverse-square law connects the two numbers.

## Slide 11: Try It: Five Magnitudes
type: quiz
title: Try It: Five Magnitudes
bullets:
  - Star A is magnitude 1; star B is magnitude 6
  - Which is brighter, and by what factor?
  - Star C is magnitude 3.5. Roughly how does it compare with star A?
  - What if two identical stars differ by 5 magnitudes? Which is farther, and by how much?
notes: A is 100 times brighter than B. C is 2.5 magnitudes fainter than A, a factor of 10. Identical stars 100 times apart in brightness: the fainter is 10 times farther, by the inverse-square law.

## Slide 12: Color Is Temperature
type: content
title: Color Is Temperature
bullets:
  - Hot stars glow blue-white; cool stars glow orange-red
  - The stove burner again, at much higher temperatures
  - Betelgeuse looks orange-red; Rigel looks blue-white, in the same constellation
  - A star's color gives its surface temperature from across the galaxy
notes: Look at Orion on the next clear night. The color difference is visible to the eye.

## Slide 13: OBAFGKM
type: content
title: OBAFGKM
bullets:
  - Stars are sorted into spectral classes O, B, A, F, G, K, M
  - From hottest (O, blue, 30,000 K and up) to coolest (M, red, about 3,000 K)
  - The Sun is a G2 star with a surface near 5,800 K
  - Each class splits into 0 to 9; G2 is a little cooler than G0
body: |
  Make up your own mnemonic. The weirder, the better you will remember it.
notes: The letters are out of alphabetical order for historical reasons. The order is by temperature.

## Slide 14: Spectral Lines as Fingerprints
type: content
title: Spectral Lines as Fingerprints
bullets:
  - A star's spectrum is crossed by dark absorption lines
  - Each element absorbs at its own set of wavelengths
  - Which lines are strong depends mostly on temperature, not on what the star is made of
  - That is how the classes were defined, and how we read a star's temperature
notes: Hydrogen lines are strongest in A stars. Too hot or too cool and they fade.

## Slide 15: Summary
type: summary
title: Summary
bullets:
  - Parallax gives distance from geometry; d = 1/p, in parsecs and arcseconds
  - 1 parsec is about 3.26 light-years; parallax only works for nearby stars
  - Magnitudes run backwards; 5 magnitudes is a factor of 100
  - Spectral classes O B A F G K M run hottest to coolest; the Sun is G2
