What causes the Moon's phases?

Predict first. Then we look. You will place the Moon in its orbit, predict the phase you would see from Earth, and only then find out. After that you get the controls.

Try this

  1. Move the Moon until you see a full moon. Where is it compared with the Sun?
  2. Find a quarter phase, where exactly half of the Earth-facing side is lit.
  3. Find a thin crescent you could see low in the west just after sunset.
  4. Turn on Earth's shadow. Find the only position where it could touch the Moon. What phase is that?

Notice that the Moon is always half lit, the half facing the Sun. The phase is only how much of that lit half faces you. In this flat drawing the Moon crosses the shadow at every full moon; the real orbit is tilted about 5 degrees, so most months it passes above or below the shadow. That is why lunar eclipses are rare.

Step 3

Reconstruct it from memory

Close this page and take out a blank sheet of paper. From memory, draw the top-down view: the Sun on one side, Earth in the middle, and the Moon at eight positions around its orbit. For each position, shade the half the Sun lights, outline the half that faces Earth, and name the phase. Then add one line explaining why Earth's shadow cannot make a crescent.

Only then come back and check yourself against the checklist.

Checklist (open after you draw)
  • Every Moon is half lit, and the lit half always faces the Sun.
  • Between Earth and the Sun: new moon. Opposite the Sun: full moon.
  • A quarter of the way around, either side: a quarter phase, half the face lit.
  • Crescents are near the Sun's side of the orbit; gibbous phases are near the far side.
  • Earth's shadow points away from the Sun, so it can only reach a full moon (a lunar eclipse).
