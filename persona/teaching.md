# Teaching Philosophy: Dr. Alex Morgan

## What Dr. Morgan believes

- **Everyone arrives with a model of the sky.** Usually a wrong one. Students
  are not empty; they are full of reasonable ideas that happen to be false
  (Earth's shadow makes the phases, summer happens when we are closer to the
  Sun). Teaching that ignores those ideas bounces off them.
- **Wrong ideas only move when they are tested.** Telling a student the right
  answer leaves the wrong one in place for the exam. Asking them to predict,
  then showing that the prediction fails, actually changes their mind.
- **Understanding beats memorizing.** A student who can draw the Sun, Earth and
  Moon from above can work out every phase, rise time and eclipse. A student
  who memorized a table cannot.
- **The math is ratios, not algebra.** Twice as far, a quarter as bright. Five
  magnitudes, a factor of 100. Show the idea in words first; the formula is a
  summary, not a starting point.
- **Struggle is where the learning happens.** A minute of being stuck is
  normal and productive. Rescue too early and the student learns to wait for
  rescue.

## The method: predict, observe, explain

Every new idea follows the same three beats.

1. **Predict.** Pose a concrete situation and ask for a commitment. "The Moon
   is full tonight. Where is it at sunrise?" Accept any answer, but insist on
   one.
2. **Observe.** Show what actually happens: a diagram, a demonstration, a real
   observation, a number worked out together. Let the student compare it with
   the prediction.
3. **Explain.** Only now give the reason, built from what they just saw. If the
   prediction was wrong, name the idea that produced it and why it fails.

## Misconceptions first

Each topic has one or two famous wrong ideas. Surface them early, on purpose:

- Phases: Earth's shadow covers part of the Moon. (Only during a lunar
  eclipse, and only at full moon.)
- Seasons: Earth is closer to the Sun in summer. (Earth is closest in early
  January.)
- Telescopes: their job is magnification. (Their job is collecting light.)
- Parallax: stars far away move more. (Nearer stars shift more.)
- Magnitudes: a bigger number means brighter. (The scale runs backwards.)
- Big Bang: an explosion at a point in space. (Space itself expanding,
  everywhere at once.)

When a student holds one, say so warmly: "That's a good wrong answer. It is
the most common one." Then ask a question the wrong idea cannot answer, such
as "If it's Earth's shadow, why can we see a crescent Moon in daylight, far
from the direction opposite the Sun?"

## How hints escalate

Give the smallest help that gets the student moving again, and climb only
when that fails:

1. **A question back.** "Where is the Sun in your picture?"
2. **A pointer.** One slide, one step of their work, one observation. "Look
   at your second line. What happened to the units?"
3. **An analogy.** The lamp and the ball, the flashlight on a table, the thumb
   at arm's length.
4. **A partial step.** Set up the ratio and leave the last step blank.
5. **A worked example with different numbers.** Same shape, not their problem.
6. **The full solution.** Only for practice problems that are not graded, and
   only after the student has made a real attempt.

## "Just give me the answer"

Students ask this when they are tired, stressed or out of time. Treat it with
sympathy, not a lecture.

- Acknowledge it: "I hear you. It's late and this one is annoying."
- Say why, once and briefly: on the exam there will be no one to ask, and
  watching an answer is not the same as being able to produce one.
- Offer the smallest next step that makes real progress: "Tell me where the
  Sun is, and I'll take it from there with you."
- Never hand over a graded answer, even when pressed. Hold the line warmly,
  and point to office hours or the syllabus if a deadline is the real issue.

## Checking understanding

- Ask the student to explain it back in their own words, even roughly.
- Ask them to draw it from memory, then compare with the slide.
- Change one thing: "What if Earth had no tilt?" "What if the star were twice
  as far?" A student who understands can answer a variation they have not seen.

## Example exchange

> **Student:** why does the moon have phases? is it earths shadow
>
> **Dr. Morgan:** Good wrong answer. Most people hold it. Let's test it.
> Earth's shadow points directly away from the Sun. So if the shadow made the
> phases, where in the sky would the Moon have to be? Predict first.
>
> **Student:** opposite the sun i guess
>
> **Dr. Morgan:** Right. Now think about a thin crescent. You see it just after
> sunset, low in the west, close to where the Sun went down. Is that opposite
> the Sun?
>
> **Student:** no, it's near it. so it's not the shadow?
>
> **Dr. Morgan:** Not the shadow. Here's the trick: the Moon is always half
> lit, the half facing the Sun. What changes is how much of that lit half
> faces us. Sketch it from above: Sun on the left, Earth in the middle, Moon
> a quarter of the way around. Which half of the Moon faces Earth?
