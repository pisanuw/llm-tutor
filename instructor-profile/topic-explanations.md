# Topic Explanations: Dr. Alex Morgan

## AST 101, Lecture 01: The Sky Above Us

- The sky behaves as if it were a sphere centered on you (a model, not a real
  shell). Zenith straight up, horizon at your feet, celestial poles where
  Earth's axis points, celestial equator above Earth's equator.
- Daily motion is Earth's rotation seen from inside: the office chair. The sky
  turns about 15 degrees per hour around the celestial pole.
- Polaris sits at an altitude equal to your latitude. Stars closer to the pole
  than that never set: circumpolar stars.
- Angles by hand at arm's length. One degree is 60 arcminutes; one arcminute
  is 60 arcseconds.

**Misconceptions:** the stars sit on a real dome at one distance; Polaris is
the brightest star (it is about the 50th); the stars themselves move nightly.

## AST 101, Lecture 02: Phases of the Moon and Eclipses

- The Moon is always half lit. The phase is how much of the lit half faces
  Earth, set by the Sun-Earth-Moon angle. The lamp and the ball.
- The cycle takes about 29.5 days. Full moon is opposite the Sun and rises at
  sunset; first quarter is 90 degrees east of the Sun and is high at sunset.
- Solar eclipses need a new moon, lunar eclipses a full moon, and both need the
  Moon at a node of its orbit, tilted about 5 degrees. So about two eclipse
  seasons a year, not an eclipse every month.
- Two tides a day: the Moon pulls the near side more than Earth's center, and
  the center more than the far side, stretching Earth into two bulges. Spring
  tides at new and full moon, when Sun and Moon line up.

**Misconceptions:** Earth's shadow causes the phases; the Moon is only up at
night; the far side is always dark; one tide a day, toward the Moon.

## AST 101, Lecture 03: Seasons

- Earth's axis is tilted 23.5 degrees and points the same way in space all
  year. In June the Northern Hemisphere leans toward the Sun.
- Tilt does two things: sunlight arrives more directly (the flashlight) and
  days are longer. Both put more energy on each square meter.
- Solstices (about June 21 and December 21): the Sun as far north or south as
  it gets. Equinoxes (about March 20 and September 22): the Sun over the
  equator, day and night about equal everywhere.
- Noon Sun altitude = 90 degrees minus latitude plus the Sun's declination
  (+23.5 in June, 0 at the equinoxes, -23.5 in December).

**Misconceptions:** distance causes the seasons (Earth is closest in early
January, and the hemispheres have opposite seasons at once); the noon Sun is
overhead everywhere; the solstice is the hottest day.

## AST 101, Lecture 04: Light and Telescopes

- Light is an electromagnetic wave: wavelength times frequency equals the
  speed of light. Shorter wavelength, higher frequency, more energy per photon.
- Order: radio, microwave, infrared, visible (red to violet), ultraviolet,
  X-ray, gamma ray. Hotter objects glow bluer.
- Brightness falls with the square of distance: twice as far, a quarter as
  bright. The flashlight beam spreading out.
- A telescope's first job is collecting light (it goes as aperture squared),
  its second is resolution (finer with bigger aperture). Magnification last.

**Misconceptions:** telescopes are for magnifying; radio waves are sound;
brightness falls in simple proportion to distance.

## AST 102, Lecture 01: Measuring the Stars

- Parallax is the thumb blink with Earth's orbit as the baseline. Distance in
  parsecs = 1 / parallax in arcseconds; one parsec is about 3.26 light-years.
- Every stellar parallax is under one arcsecond, so the method reaches only
  relatively nearby stars, even from space.
- Magnitudes run backwards, and five magnitudes is a factor of 100. Absolute
  magnitude is how bright a star would look from 10 parsecs.
- Spectral classes O B A F G K M run hottest (blue) to coolest (red). The Sun
  is a G2 star at about 5,800 K.

**Misconceptions:** farther stars shift more; a bigger magnitude is brighter;
red stars are the hot ones, because red means hot on a faucet.

## AST 102, Lecture 02: The Lives of Stars

- Plot luminosity against temperature, hot on the left. Most stars fall on the
  main sequence. Giants are cool but luminous because they are huge; white
  dwarfs are hot but dim because they are tiny.
- Main-sequence stars fuse hydrogen into helium in the core. Gravity pulls in,
  pressure pushes out, and the two balance. Mass sets the position.
- Massive stars burn hotter and much faster, so they die young.
- Endpoints by initial mass: below about 8 solar masses, a white dwarf; above
  that, a supernova leaving a neutron star or, for the most massive, a black
  hole.

**Misconceptions:** stars burn like a fire; stars slide along the main
sequence as they age; the Sun will become a black hole.

## AST 102, Lecture 03: Galaxies

- The Milky Way is a disk about 100,000 light-years across, with a bulge and a
  halo. The Sun is in the disk about 26,000 light-years from the center, so we
  see the disk edge-on as a band (inside the forest).
- Spirals have gas, dust and young blue stars in their arms; ellipticals hold
  old reddish stars and little gas; irregulars have no clear shape.
- Outer planets orbit slower, but outer stars in a galaxy orbit about as fast
  as inner ones: a flat rotation curve. The ball on a string says that means
  unseen mass, dark matter, about five times the ordinary matter.

**Misconceptions:** the band is a cloud in our air; we are at the galaxy's
center; dark matter is dark gas or dust.

## AST 102, Lecture 04: The Expanding Universe

- Redshift z = (observed wavelength - rest wavelength) / rest wavelength. For
  small z, recession speed is about z times the speed of light (the siren).
- Hubble's law: v = H0 x d, with H0 about 70 km/s per megaparsec, so d = v / H0.
- Space itself stretches, like raisin bread: every galaxy sees the others
  receding, and there is no center. Running it backward gives an age near
  1/H0, about 14 billion years (the measured value is 13.8).
- Evidence: the expansion, the cosmic microwave background (a 2.7 K glow from
  about 380,000 years after the start), and the amounts of light elements.

**Misconceptions:** the Big Bang was an explosion at a point in space; we are
at the center of the expansion; galaxies themselves are expanding.
