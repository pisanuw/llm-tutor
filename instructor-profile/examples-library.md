# Examples Library: Dr. Alex Morgan

The same few objects come back all term: a flashlight, a lamp, a ball, your
own hand. Reuse them; students remember what they did with their own thumb.

## AST 101: The Night Sky

**The celestial sphere: the umbrella.** An open umbrella over your head with
stars painted inside. The handle points at the celestial pole; turn it and
every star circles the pole. The rim is your horizon.

**Daily motion: the office chair.** Spin slowly and the room "goes around"
you, though nothing in it moved. Earth turns west to east, so the sky seems to
turn east to west, 15 degrees per hour.

**Circumpolar stars: the Big Dipper.** From 45 degrees north, Polaris is 45
degrees up and the Big Dipper never sets.

**Angles: your outstretched hand.** At arm's length: little finger about 1
degree (it covers the half-degree full Moon), three fingers about 5, a fist
about 10, a spread hand about 20. The Big Dipper's pointer stars are about 5
degrees apart, half a fist.

**Moon phases: the lamp and the ball.** A bare lamp in a dark room is the Sun,
a white ball held at arm's length is the Moon, your head is Earth. Turn slowly
in a circle: the ball is always half lit, and you watch the whole cycle with
no shadow from your head, except when the ball passes directly behind it (a
lunar eclipse).

**Eclipses: two tilted hula hoops.** Earth's orbit and the Moon's are hoops
tilted about 5 degrees. They cross at two nodes. An eclipse needs a new or full
moon at a node, which happens about twice a year.

**Tides: three runners.** The Moon pulls the near side hardest, the center
less, the far side least. Three runners on one rope pulled from the front: the
group stretches. Earth stretches into two bulges.

**Seasons: the flashlight on the table.** Straight down: a small, bright
circle. Tilted: a big, dim oval. Same light, more area. Summer is the straight
beam. For skeptics: Earth is closest to the Sun in early January.

**The Sun's path: the window patch.** Mark where noon sunlight from a window
lands on the floor once a month. Near the window in summer (high Sun), deep in
the room in winter (low Sun).

**The spectrum: the piano keyboard.** A keyboard miles long, of which our eyes
hear about one octave, red to violet. Radio is far to the left, X-rays far to
the right.

**Inverse-square law: the flashlight and the streetlights.** At twice the
distance a flashlight's light covers four times the area: a quarter as bright.
Of two identical streetlights, the one three times farther looks one ninth as
bright.

**Telescopes: the bucket in the rain.** A bucket twice as wide catches four
times the rain. Magnification only enlarges what the bucket caught; a big
image of a little light is a big blur.

## AST 102: Stars and Galaxies

**Parallax: the thumb blink.** Thumb at arm's length, blink one eye, then the
other: it jumps against the far wall, and jumps more when closer. Earth's orbit
is the distance between our eyes; a nearby star is the thumb.

**Parallax numbers: Proxima Centauri.** Parallax 0.77 arcseconds, so d = 1/0.77,
about 1.3 parsecs or 4.2 light-years. The biggest stellar parallax there is,
and about the width of a coin seen from 5 kilometers.

**Magnitudes: golf scores.** Lower is better. Magnitude 1 is 100 times brighter
than magnitude 6; each step is about 2.5 times.

**Spectral classes: the stove burner.** A burner glows dull red, then orange,
then yellow-white as it heats. M stars are about 3,000 K, the Sun (G) about
5,800 K, O stars 30,000 K and up.

**Stellar lifetimes: the gas-guzzler.** A massive star has a bigger tank and a
much bigger engine. Ten solar masses lasts tens of millions of years; the Sun
lasts about 10 billion.

**The Milky Way: inside the forest.** In a forest you see trees all around the
horizon and open sky overhead. Inside the galaxy's disk we see a band of stars.

**Rotation curves: the ball on a string.** To swing a ball faster at the same
radius you must pull harder. Orbit speed tells you the pull, and the pull tells
you the mass inside. Fast orbits far out mean mass we cannot see.

**Redshift: the passing siren.** Its pitch drops as it moves away; the waves
are stretched. A receding galaxy's light is stretched toward red.

**Expansion: the raisin bread.** As dough rises every raisin moves away from
every other, and a raisin twice as far moves twice as fast. No raisin is the
center.
